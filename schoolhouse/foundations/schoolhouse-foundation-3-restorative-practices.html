Restorative Practices Basics

A framework for thinking about harm, repair, and community in a classroom — and what it asks of a teacher.

The central idea

Restorative practices start from a single premise: when someone causes harm, the relevant question is not what rule was broken and what punishment follows, but who was affected, how, and what needs to happen to make it right.

In a school context, this shifts the purpose of discipline from consequences to repair — and it changes who’s involved. The student who caused harm, the student who was harmed, and the community that holds them both are all part of the conversation.

“Restorative approaches shift the central question from ‘what rule was broken and who broke it’ to ‘who was harmed and what do they need.’”
Zehr, 2002

What it’s not

Restorative practices are often misread as “no consequences” or “saying sorry and moving on.” Neither is accurate.

A restorative approach can include significant consequences — the difference is that consequences are tied to repair rather than punishment, and the student has some role in determining what repair looks like. A student who breaks something fixes it. A student who excludes someone finds a way to include them. The consequence is calibrated to the harm.

Accountability in a restorative frame means the student understands what they did, who it affected, and what they’re doing about it — not that they received a pre-set consequence.

Proactive, not just reactive

Most of what restorative practice accomplishes in a classroom happens before there’s a conflict. The community-building piece — circle practice, shared norms, regular relational conversations — builds the relationships and shared agreements that make repair possible when something goes wrong.

A repair conversation works because the people in it already have some relationship. A class meeting can metabolize conflict because students already know how to speak to each other in that structure. The proactive work is what makes the reactive work possible.

The classroom teacher’s role

Full restorative practice — formal conferences, trained facilitators, circle processes — is a school-level system. A classroom teacher isn’t running formal conferences. What a classroom teacher can do is smaller and more constant: building enough relationship that repair conversations are possible, speaking restoratively when something goes wrong, and designing a culture where mistakes don’t end membership.

The repair conversation — a two-to-five-minute structured exchange — is the classroom-level tool. It’s not a formal process. It’s a habit of asking the right questions.
