Working Memory

The mind’s active workspace — small, easily overloaded, and where all actual thinking happens.

Working memory is where information is held and manipulated in the moment of thinking. It is small — most adults can hold roughly four chunks of information at once — and when it’s full, nothing else goes in. This makes it the bottleneck for learning: instruction that overloads working memory doesn’t slow learning, it stops it.

Automaticity is the relief valve. When a procedure has been rehearsed enough to run without conscious direction, it stops consuming working memory. This is why classroom routines are instructionally relevant, not merely managerial: a student who can get materials, find their seat, and begin the entry task without thinking about any of those steps has working memory available for the content that follows. Routines free the workspace for the thing that’s supposed to go there.
