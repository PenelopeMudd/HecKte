Mental & Behavioral Health

Can the student tolerate being here? The other four barriers say I can’t come. This one says I can’t tolerate being there — and that sentence has the building in it. This is where barrier turns into experience, and it is the place the two halves of the room actually meet.

The participation question

Is being in this building survivable for this student today? Not does the student have a diagnosis. Whether the experience of being here exceeds what they can currently regulate — and whether any of what they cannot tolerate is something the building made.

What you can do here — and what you can’t

Attendance is a requirement the school cannot produce. A school does not deliver clinical treatment, and multimodal family-inclusive CBT — the thing that works — requires resources most buildings do not have.

But this factor breaks the pattern of the other four in one specific way. The other barriers originate outside the building. Part of this one originates inside it. A student avoiding an aversive social situation is avoiding a social situation that is happening here. That does not make anxiety the school’s fault; it does mean the school is a variable in this factor rather than the backdrop it is in factors 1 through 4.

InfluenceWhether the family is connected to clinical care that exists · whether the counselor is reachable without an incident first · whether a partial day is treated as a success or a failure · whether the specific aversive thing has been located rather than assumed
RedesignWhat re-entry after an anxious absence requires · whether a returning student must account for where they were, in front of anyone · gradual and partial return as an available option rather than an exception · which transitions, hallways, and hours the building’s own schedule makes unsurvivable · whether the response to avoidance is exclusionary

What sits outside this factor: diagnosis, treatment, medication, whether a family can access a clinician at all. You connect students to those; other systems resolve them.

And a boundary in the other direction. The clinical frame locates the mechanism in the child — accurately, for clinical populations. It can also convert an appropriate response to a real environment into an individual deficit. A student anxious about a building with unchecked bullying is not dysregulated. Before treating avoidance as pathology, check whether the thing being avoided is real.

You are not a clinician. You cannot treat an anxiety disorder, and a student whose distress exceeds what this building can hold needs care you are not able to provide.

What you own is whether the thing being avoided is something you built. And whether coming back, after being gone for a reason nobody wants to say out loud, costs anything at all.

This is the one barrier where checking the building comes before referring the child. Not instead of. Before.

Read through Restorative Practices

(This room’s lens. See Restorative Practices — Basics.)

This is the factor the lens reaches furthest into, because the aversive experience is often relational and often local.

Absence here is consent withdrawn under conditions the building partly set. The developmental literature names four functions: avoiding negative affect, escaping an aversive social situation, seeking caregiver attention, and accessing something better outside school. Two of those four point directly at the building. An agreement a student cannot tolerate keeping is a term problem, not a compliance problem.

Which is why the return is the entire intervention this lens supports. Return after an anxiety-driven absence is the hardest return there is — the longer out, the higher the cost of walking back in, and the cost is precisely social. Absence generates its own next absence, and it does so faster in this factor than in any other.

Make return cheap. Someone who says good to see you and nothing else. A way back in that does not require accounting for where you were in front of a room. A student who returns to a demand for justification learns that coming back costs something — and in this factor, that cost is the exact currency they are already short of.

The escalation, named: the standard response to a student who avoids school is removal from school. Suspension, court referral, exclusion. Responding to intolerable presence with enforced absence is the loop in its purest form.

By grade band

Attendance agency moves from the household into the student. This factor changes hands as it moves.

The household still executes arrival — and this is the band where separation is the mechanism. A child in distress at the door is not refusing school so much as refusing the separation, and the caregiver is the one standing in it.

Agency sits with the caregiver, under pressure. A caregiver facing a screaming five-year-old and a shift start is making a decision the school will later read as a household choice. What the building owns is the handoff at the door — who receives that child, whether the same person receives them every day, and whether the caregiver has anyone here to talk to about it.

Somatic presentation dominates and it is genuinely ambiguous. Stomachaches, headaches, real sensations with a school-shaped trigger. The child is not fabricating and also not simply ill, and sorting it is largely not possible from the office.

Agency is split and unstable. The child now generates the resistance; the household still adjudicates it. What the building owns is whether the nurse’s office is a place a child can land and return from, rather than a one-way exit. (Lee (2023): students with frequent partial-day absences seek the nurse out for comfort and support — the access point already exists.)

The handoff, and it lands hardest here. Social aversion becomes primary exactly as the student gains the ability to decline. Who they would face, what they would have to explain about being gone, what happened before they left.

Agency moves to the student, over a decision driven by what the building feels like. This is the band where the barrier is most clearly also an experience — and where a purely clinical reading will miss it, because the thing being avoided may be entirely real and entirely local.

The student decides, and the reasons are hers. Anxiety, depression, and a calculation that the day costs more than it returns. Clinical need is highest and clinical access is not.

Where agency sits: with the student, fully. One adult who knows her situation and does not moralize about it is the version that works here, and routing through the household may be exactly what breaks it. The bar is that the building contains at least one person for whom her absence registers as a specific loss.

Evidence

Mental health has overtaken physical health as the leading health driver of absenteeism. Anxiety, depression, and stress now top the list at school-based health centers, surpassing asthma and dental pain, which were historically first. (Pediatrics lens, convergent discipline analysis.) Demonstrated as a shift in presentation at the settings that report it.

Anxiety disorders are present in 50–60% of school-refusal cases. (School refusal literature.) Demonstrated.

Kearney’s functional model (SRAS-R) is the clinical standard: four functional reasons a child cannot attend — avoiding negative affect, escaping aversive social situations, caregiver attention, and reinforcement outside school — each requiring a different response. Demonstrated as a clinical framework. Its value here is diagnostic of which problem is running, not of what a school should do.

Maynard (2018), meta-analysis: interventions move attendance but not anxiety. Demonstrated, and it is the most important line in this row. Getting a student back in the building is not the same as making the building tolerable, and the field’s own meta-analytic evidence says so.

Egger, Costello & Angold (2003), Great Smoky Mountains Study: anxious school refusal predicts later depression at OR 13; truancy predicts conduct disorder at OR 7.4. Demonstrated, and it establishes something structural — these are two different populations with two different trajectories, and the attendance record does not distinguish them.

Intensive, multimodal, family-inclusive CBT increases attendance — and requires clinical resources most schools do not have. (Developmental psychology lens.) Demonstrated, and largely unavailable, which is the practical shape of this factor.

That school-side re-entry design affects anxious return — no study located. Proposed. It follows from the restorative reading and from Maynard’s finding that attendance and anxiety move separately, and no found work tests it.

Honest limit: two limits, and they cut against each other. The clinical literature is strong and its instruments only reach the child — the unit assessed, the scale scored, the treatment delivered, and the outcome measured all sit on the student, in a field that explicitly disclaims child fault and has reformed its own terminology to say so. What a field can measure ends up determining what it holds responsible. Read the clinical evidence knowing its aperture. And nothing in this evidence base establishes that a school changing its own environment reduces avoidance. The claim that part of this factor originates inside the building is the room’s structural reading, not a demonstrated finding. It is the most testable proposition in the barriers half and it has not been tested.
