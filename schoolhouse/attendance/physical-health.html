Physical Health

Is the body able to be here today — and what happens when it wasn’t? This factor covers when the body is the reason. Not the regulation, not the household’s bandwidth, not the building. Those have their own rows.

What is it

Health-related absence covers more ground than “out sick” usually implies:

  • Acute illness — the cold, the flu, the fever. The most legitimized absence and, after recovery, the most underexamined one.
  • Chronic conditions — asthma, eczema, epilepsy, diabetes, GI conditions. Managed well, they cost fewer days; managed poorly, they cost many.
  • Dental pain — often invisible in absence records but not in the student’s mouth.
  • Disability-related health absence — counted here when the body is the primary reason; the design authority differs from other health absence.
  • Partial days through the nurse’s office — a student who leaves for an hour and returns is present by count. The pattern still says something.

What this factor doesn’t include: mental and behavioral health. That’s Factor 5 — and it’s now the leading driver of school absence. Physical health is what most people meant, historically, by “out sick.”

What sits outside this factor entirely: whether the household has a pediatrician, insurance, or transportation to a clinic. That’s Factor 3. This row is about what happens when the body couldn’t come — and what the school owns on the day after.

What did we learn

Asthma alone accounts for roughly 13–14 million missed school days per year in the United States. (Public health literature; widely reproduced.) Demonstrated as a magnitude.

Home-based multi-trigger asthma intervention returns approximately 12.3 school days per student per year. (Community Preventive Services Task Force, 2011.) Demonstrated — and the finding’s location matters: the effective intervention happened in the home, not the school. The school’s role in that finding is connection, not treatment.

5.8% of children ages 5–17 have health-related chronic absence. Among children with disabilities: 14.8%. Among children without: 4.4%. (NCHS, 2024.) Demonstrated.

Roughly 3 million children missed 15 or more school days per year for health reasons in 2022. (CDC.) Demonstrated.

The relationship is bidirectional. Poor health causes absence; chronic absence predicts worse long-term health outcomes. (AAP.) Demonstrated. This is a loop — absence doesn’t just follow health; it also shapes it.

Students with frequent partial-day absences actively seek out school nurses as comfort and support, creating an intervention opening before chronic absenteeism is established. (Lee, 2023.) Qualitative — establishes the access point, not a prediction. Reasoned as a lever.

Re-entry design affecting subsequent attendance — no study isolated this directly. Proposed. It follows from what is demonstrated about exclusionary responses and from what the re-entry experience costs a returning student.

Honest limit: the literature is strong on magnitude and nearly silent on what a school does about it. It establishes that illness removes millions of days and that clinical intervention — which happens outside the school — returns some of them. No study located isolates a school-side practice that changes a health-related attendance trajectory.

What do we know so far

What the school has full authority over is the return. Not the illness — that happened. Whether the pile waiting for a returning student is climbable. Whether the person who asks about those days wants a justification or wants to know what happened. “What happened” opens a channel that “why weren’t you here” closes.

The school’s standard response to illness-related absence — the note demanded, the missed work sent home in a stack — is built for the machinery of defiance, not for a lapsed agreement that nobody chose. A student who comes back to a demand for justification learns that returning costs something. Absence then generates its own next absence without anyone intending it.

K–2. The household holds every call. A six-year-old doesn’t assess their own symptoms or decide whether today is a stay-home day. That decision happens at 6:45 in the morning with incomplete information and often a work shift attached. A caregiver with one non-summoning contact at the school has somewhere to ask is this a stay-home? A caregiver whose only contact is a day-count letter doesn’t.

3–5. Stomachaches and headaches are frequently both — a genuine physical sensation with a school-shaped trigger underneath it. Sorting them isn’t the school’s job. What it owns is whether the nurse’s office is a place a student can go without it becoming an event. Lee (2023) found the office already functions as a relational access point in schools that didn’t design it that way. Whether that’s intentional is an open question.

6–8. The student begins managing their own condition — and managing it in front of peers. An inhaler used in a hallway. A device that beeps. A bathroom pass that comes too often. The redesign question is whether the building’s routines make a condition manageable without publicity.

Emerging questions

  • Does reducing the school’s own cost of attending — lighter re-entry loads, partial-day credit, a nurse’s office that doesn’t produce an event — change attendance patterns after health-related absence?
  • When the school nurse functions as a relational access point, does intentionality change the outcome — or is the access point effective regardless of whether it was designed?
  • The bidirectionality finding (AAP) means absence worsens health, which increases absence. At what point in that loop does a school-based intervention become possible?

The boundary

You cannot make a sick child well, and you should not try to make a sick child come. That absence was correct.

What you own is the day after. Whether returning costs anything. Whether the pile waiting for her is climbable. Whether the person who asks about it wants an explanation or wants to know what happened.

Where the school’s design authority is thinnest, its authority over the re-entry is total. That is a small claim and an entirely keepable one.
