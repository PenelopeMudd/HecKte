School Climate & Safety

What is it like to be here, and does being here cost anything? This is where the school becomes the variable. The barriers ask what is happening in the student’s life. This one asks what is happening in this building — and it is the first factor whose answer is authored almost entirely inside the walls. Climate is a designed condition, not a weather report.

The participation question

Is a day here survivable? Not does the student feel safe as a sentiment. Whether the hallway, the cafeteria, the bathroom, and the first ten minutes of a period are places she can enter without paying a price — and whether the adults’ response to conflict makes her more or less willing to walk in tomorrow.

What you can do here — and what you can’t

Attendance is a requirement the school cannot produce. Every input to a child arriving happens somewhere else. But climate is different from the other factors in one specific way: the conditions are manufactured on site. Who supervises the hallway, what happens after a fight, whether a report goes anywhere — those are decisions, and decisions have authors.

What you personally hold is smaller than the climate and larger than nothing. You own your share of the pattern, and the pattern is what students read.

ObserveWhere students slow down or route around · who eats alone · which periods generate the nurse visits and the bathroom passes · who leaves after third hour and comes back tomorrow
InfluenceWhether a reported conflict produces a response the student can see · whether the adult response to a hard moment is repair or removal · whether the same behavior gets the same answer from you on a bad day
RedesignSupervision of unowned space — halls, cafeteria, bus line · what happens after a conflict · re-entry after suspension · transition-year structures at the 5→6 and 8→9 seams

What sits outside this factor: the neighborhood, the ride, the household, the body. Those are real and usually larger. You connect students to those; other systems resolve them. And you do not own the whole building’s climate — but you author a legible piece of it, and students generalize from the piece they meet.

You cannot produce attendance, and you do not own the building’s climate. Nobody in the building owns all of it — climate is the sum of many people’s ordinary decisions, most of them not yours.

What you own is your share of the precedent. What happens in your room when something goes wrong, and what a student walks back into afterward.

That is a smaller claim than most climate initiatives make, and it is one you can actually keep.

Read through Restorative Practices

(This room’s lens. See Restorative Practices — Basics.)

Climate is the standing record of how conflict gets handled here. Students are not surveying their feelings. They are reading precedent: what happened the last time something went wrong, to whom, and whether anyone repaired it. That record is what they consult at 6:45 in the morning.

Which makes exclusion the central design question, not a discipline sidebar. The field’s standard escalation for a student who is missing school is removal from school — suspension, court referral. Klevan (2021) reports the pattern restorative practice was built against: exclusionary discipline increases future misbehavior and dropout risk. Removal answers an absence with an absence.

Safety and accountability are the same commitment, not a trade. Connecticut’s statutory definition of restorative practices carries both inside one sentence — building high-quality relationships, holding students accountable for challenging behavior, and repairing and reintegrating. A student who was harmed needs the harm addressed; a student who caused harm needs a road back. A building that does only the second is unsafe. A building that does only the first is where absence starts.

And the re-entry is the climate signal. After a suspension, after a fight, after a bad week — what a student walks back into tells her what this place does with people who have gone wrong. That is the same mechanic as the return after absence, applied to conflict.

By grade band

Attendance agency moves from the household into the student. This factor changes hands as it moves.

Agency sits with the household, so climate is read by the adult who brings the child. A six-year-old cannot decide to stay home over a hallway. But a caregiver who finds the office cold, the front desk suspicious, or the last phone call accusatory can decide the trip isn’t worth it. The climate that matters at this band is the climate of the entry — the door, the desk, the tone of the first contact.

The child’s own reading is real but narrow: one adult, one room, whether it is loud, whether it is predictable.

Both, and peer safety becomes legible. The child can now name what happened at recess and can produce a stomachache the morning after. Household agency still executes arrival; the child’s report of the day is what shapes the household’s willingness to push. Unowned space starts doing the damage — the playground, the line, the bus — because that is where the adult pattern is thinnest.

The handoff, and the transition seam. The student can now decline: not board the bus, get dropped off and walk. Attendance commonly declines across the elementary→middle transition, and this is the band where avoiding a person becomes a reason to avoid a building.

The move is shrinking the unowned space. Named adults on the hall, on the bus line, in the cafeteria. A conflict report that visibly produces something. A student who knows where the trouble is and knows nothing happens about it has been told what this place is.

The student decides, and she prices the day. Climate now competes with a shift, a sibling, a bed. A building that costs something to enter loses that comparison quickly, and she does not have to explain the decision to anyone.

Partial-day absence is the tell here. She is in the building and not in the room — which reads on the record as present. If the climate problem is one period, one hallway, one adult, the attendance data will not surface it. Someone has to go look.

Evidence

Klevan (2021) — whole-school restorative practice is associated with improved climate and connectedness and reduced exclusionary discipline; exclusionary discipline increases future misbehavior and dropout risk. (Synthesis brief — it reports these findings rather than producing them.)

Connecticut — restorative practices legally defined in statute, with adoption of the School Climate Policy and a restorative response policy mandatory as of July 1, 2025, plus a mandatory school climate survey of students, employees, and families in 2025–26. Chronic absenteeism fell 20% → 17.7% in 2023–24. A working example, not a controlled demonstration — nobody has isolated the climate or RP contribution.

And Connecticut has both halves and has not joined them. The climate survey produces connectedness data; the attendance reporting produces absence data. Nothing yet connects the two at state scale. The most direct available test of this factor exists as two datasets nobody has merged.

A climate problem located in one class period leaves a student present in the building and absent from that room — a pattern an annual attendance figure reports as attendance.

Footnote: Whitney & Liu (2017), AERA Open 3(2) — one large California district, grades 6–12, 2007–13, n>50,000. Counting part-day absence raised chronic absenteeism there from 9% to 24%. Secondary only, one district, pre-pandemic, and a critique of day-based counting rather than Missouri’s hour-based PAR.

Honest limit: the association between bullying, perceived safety, and attendance is widely reported and not causally established — no study isolates a climate change as the cause of an attendance change. The specific claims that safety and fair discipline track attendance are drawn from the field’s descriptive consensus rather than from a controlled demonstration; where this row states them, it is reporting a pattern, not a proof. Treat this factor as structurally strong and empirically correlational.
