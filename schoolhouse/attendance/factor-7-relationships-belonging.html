Relationships & Belonging

Is there anyone here who would notice they were gone? Of the nine factors, this is the one a school-based relationship-holder can actually move — and the one that buffers the others. A student can face poverty, illness, and anxiety and still come if someone here knows them.

The participation question

Would being missed be true? Not does the student like school. Whether their absence registers as a loss to a specific person, and whether they know it would.

What you can do here — and what you can’t

Attendance is a requirement the school cannot produce. Every input to a child arriving happens somewhere else — a house, a car, a body, someone’s decision at 6:45 in the morning. No one in the building can make a student come. That is not a personal failure, and a student’s absence is not a verdict on the adult who noticed it.

What the school can do is redesign the friction between the house and the classroom. This factor is the largest piece of that you personally hold.

ObserveWho has no one · who arrives and speaks to nobody · who is absent without anyone remarking on it
InfluenceWhether a specific adult knows a specific student · whether the student knows that adult noticed · whether return after absence is welcomed or interrogated
RedesignAdvisory structures · who greets whom · what happens on the first day back · which adult owns which students by name

What sits outside this factor: transportation, health, housing, household capacity. Those are real and they are usually larger. You connect students to those; other systems resolve them. Saying so is not lowering the bar — it’s putting the requirement back where it belongs.

You cannot produce attendance. Nobody in the building can — the requirement is one the school makes and cannot fill by itself. A student’s absence is not your failure.

What you can do is make sure that when they are gone, someone notices, and when they come back, it costs them nothing.

That is a smaller claim than most attendance programs make, and it is one you can actually keep.

Read through Restorative Practices

(This room’s lens. See Restorative Practices — Basics.)

Absence is a lapsed agreement, not a violation. The school requires attendance and can only obtain it by consent. So a student who stops coming has withdrawn from an agreement — sometimes deliberately, sometimes because the conditions collapsed. Either reading calls for repair, not enforcement.

Which is why the return matters more than the absence. The restorative question is what happened, not why weren’t you here — the second invites a defense, exactly as “why did you do that” does. And a student who returns to a demand for justification learns that coming back costs something.

Repair over exclusion, stated plainly: the field’s standard escalation for missing school is removal from school — suspension for truancy, court referral that takes a parent off work. Responding to a lapsed agreement with more exclusion is the move restorative practice shows produces more of what it’s meant to correct.

And relationship is the mechanism, not the sentiment. It’s the channel a return travels on. A student with no one here has nothing to come back to, only somewhere to be required.

By grade band

Attendance agency moves from the household into the student. This factor changes hands as it moves.

The relationship that matters is with the household. A six-year-old has no say in whether they arrive; someone else decides, dresses, feeds, drives. Knowing the child is not enough — knowing the adult who brings them is the work. A caregiver who has one warm, non-summoning contact at the school is more likely to get a child there than one whose only contact is a letter about absences.

Connecticut’s home-visiting program saw its largest attendance gains in kindergarten and the early grades — the bands where household agency is total.

Both, and the child begins to have preferences. They can drag, resist, produce a stomachache that’s half real. The relationship with the household still executes arrival; the relationship with the child determines whether they want to be delivered. Roles in the room’s operation start to matter — a student who runs something is expected in a way attendance records don’t capture.

The handoff. The student can now decline — not get on the bus, get dropped off and leave. Social reasons become primary: who they’d face, what they’d have to explain about being gone. Absence starts generating its own next absence, because returning gets harder the longer you’re out.

The move is making return cheap. Someone who says good to see you and nothing else. A way back in that doesn’t require accounting for where you were.

The student decides, and the reasons change kind — work shifts, siblings to watch, a calculation that the day isn’t worth what it costs. Relationship still buffers, but it has to be with her, and going through the household may be exactly what breaks it.

One adult who knows her situation and doesn’t moralize about it is the version that works here. The bar is that the school contains at least one person for whom her absence is a specific loss rather than a data point.

Evidence

Connecticut — restorative practices written into statute (mandatory July 2025), plus LEAP, a relationship-building home-visiting program running since 2021 in the districts with the highest chronic absenteeism. Chronic absenteeism fell 20% → 17.7% in 2023–24, greatest gains in the early grades. A working example, not a controlled demonstration — nobody has isolated the relational contribution.

Klevan (2021) — whole-school restorative practice is associated with improved climate and connectedness and reduced exclusionary discipline; exclusionary discipline increases future misbehavior and dropout risk. (Synthesis brief — it reports these findings rather than producing them.) Demonstrated as association.

Belonging conditions motivation — students engage where they feel accepted; belonging is a precondition, not a reward. (Deans for Impact, Science of Learning 2nd ed., Q4.) Demonstrated as a mechanism.

Honest limit: no study isolates “a student had one trusted adult” as a cause of attendance. What exists is consistent association across climate, connectedness, and attendance, plus one state-scale working example. Treat this as well-supported and not proven.
