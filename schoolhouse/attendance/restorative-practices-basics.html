Restorative Practices

The lens. Restorative practice treats harm as a break in relationship, and treats repair as the work. It asks what happened and what it will take to make things right — and it holds accountability and reintegration together, in the same move.

Connecticut wrote that into law: evidence and research-based system-level practices focused on building high-quality relationships in the school community, holding students accountable for challenging behavior, and ensuring students have a role in repairing relationships and reintegrating into the school community.

Worth noting what’s inside that definition. The standard objection — restorative means no consequences — is answered in the statutory language itself.

Why this is the lens for attendance

Absence is a lapsed agreement.

The school requires attendance and can obtain it only by consent — every input to a child arriving happens somewhere else. So a student who stops coming has withdrawn from an agreement, sometimes deliberately, sometimes because the conditions under it collapsed. Either reading calls for repair.

Which is why the return matters more than the absence. The restorative question is what happened, not why weren’t you here — the second invites a defense, exactly as why did you do that does. A student who returns to a demand for justification learns that coming back costs something.

And it names what the field’s standard response actually is. The usual escalation for missing school is removal from school: suspension for truancy, a court referral that takes a parent off work. Responding to a lapsed agreement with more exclusion is the move restorative practice shows produces more of what it means to correct.

Relationship as mechanism

Relationship here is the channel a return travels on, not a sentiment. A student with no one in the building has nothing to come back to — only somewhere they are required to be.

That is why the factor rows keep returning to the same two questions: whether a specific adult knows a specific student, and whether the student knows that adult noticed.

What this lens does not cover

Restorative practice has little to say about transportation, housing, illness, or household capacity. Those determine whether attendance is possible; this lens reads whether it is chosen.

The barriers half of the factor list is outside what this lens explains. Saying so keeps the reading honest rather than stretching one framework across a problem it doesn’t reach.

Evidence

Klevan (2021) — whole-school restorative practice is associated with improved climate, connectedness, and reduced exclusionary discipline; exclusionary discipline in turn increases future misbehavior and dropout risk. (A synthesis brief — it reports these findings rather than producing them.) Demonstrated as association.

Connecticut — restorative practice in statute, mandatory July 2025, alongside LEAP, a relationship-building home-visiting program in the districts with the highest chronic absenteeism. Chronic absenteeism fell 20% → 17.7%, with the greatest gains in kindergarten and the early grades. A working example, not a controlled demonstration — post-COVID attendance moved in many places for many reasons.

Honest limit: nobody has isolated restorative practice’s contribution to attendance. Connecticut is a working example, not a controlled demonstration. This lens is chosen because it fits the shape of the problem, not because a trial established it.
