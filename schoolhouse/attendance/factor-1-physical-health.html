Physical Health

Is the body able to be here today — and what happens when it wasn’t? A connect factor, not a fix factor: the school does not treat asthma, fill a cavity, or manage a chronic condition. What it owns entirely is the return.

The participation question

When the body could not be here, did the school make coming back cheap? Not was the absence legitimate. The health event happened. The question is what the building did with the days on either side of it.

What you can do here — and what you can’t

Attendance is a requirement the school cannot produce. Every input to a child arriving happens somewhere else — and in this factor it happens inside the child’s body, which no adult in the building controls.

Illness is the most legitimized absence and the least examined one. A student can lose fifteen days a year to asthma and never once be asked what happened in a way that leads anywhere. The absence gets excused, and excused is where the inquiry stops.

ObserveWho is out repeatedly for the same reason · who is in the nurse’s office often and briefly · who leaves at the same hour · who has a diagnosed condition on file and a pattern that matches it
InfluenceWhether the family knows what services exist · whether a school-based health center, mobile dental clinic, or asthma program is connected to this particular child · whether the nurse is a channel or a checkpoint
RedesignRe-entry after illness · what makework a returning student is handed · how absences are communicated to the family · whether the nurse’s office is reachable without a cost · what a partial day is allowed to count as

What sits outside this factor: diagnosis, treatment, insurance, whether the family has a pediatrician at all. You connect students to those; you do not fix them. Pediatrics is reactive by design — it responds when a family seeks care, which means the students least likely to attend are also least likely to be seen.

You cannot make a sick child well, and you should not try to make a sick child come. That absence was correct.

What you own is the day after. Whether returning costs anything. Whether the pile waiting for her is climbable. Whether the person who asks about it wants an explanation or wants to know what happened.

Where the school’s design authority is thinnest, its authority over the re-entry is total. That is a small claim and an entirely keepable one.

Read through Restorative Practices

The lens reaches the return, not the illness. Restorative practice has nothing to say about asthma. It has a great deal to say about the day after.

The agreement lapsed for a reason nobody chose. That makes this the clearest case in the room: there is no violation to respond to, no consent withdrawn, only conditions that collapsed. And schools still routinely respond to it with the machinery built for defiance — a note demanded, a stack of missed work, a letter home counting days.

What happened, not why weren’t you here. A returning student who is asked what happened can say my inhaler ran out and the clinic is Tuesdays only — which is actionable. A student asked to justify an absence produces a note and nothing else. The first question opens a channel; the second closes one.

Repair over accumulation. The exclusionary move here is not suspension — it is the pile. Five days out returns as five days of work due at once, and a student who cannot climb that pile learns that the cost of returning exceeds the cost of staying out. Absence then generates its own next absence, without anyone intending it.

By grade band

Attendance agency moves from the household into the student. This factor changes hands as it moves.

The household holds all of it. A six-year-old does not assess their own symptoms, does not decide whether today is a stay-home day, and does not schedule the appointment. The judgment call belongs entirely to a caregiver, often made at 6:45 in the morning with incomplete information and a work shift attached to it.

This is where health absence is most concentrated and most invisible — early-grade chronic absence runs high and is heavily illness-coded. The reachable adult is the caregiver. A caregiver who has one non-summoning contact at the school has somewhere to ask is this a stay-home? A caregiver whose only contact is a letter about day counts does not.

The child starts producing symptoms, and some of them are real. Stomachaches and headaches at this band are frequently both — a genuine physical sensation with a school-shaped trigger underneath it. Sorting them is not the school’s job and mostly not possible.

What the school owns is that the nurse’s office is a place a child can go without it becoming an event. A student who is sent home every time they report a stomachache learns one thing; a student who can sit for twenty minutes and return to class learns another. Students with frequent partial-day absences seek out the school nurse for comfort and support — the office is already functioning as a relational access point, whether the building designed it that way or not.

The student begins negotiating their own body. They can conceal a condition they find embarrassing — asthma, eczema, a dental problem, anything visible. They can also use illness, and both happen in the same student in the same month.

Managing a chronic condition in front of peers is the new variable. An inhaler used in a hallway, a bathroom pass that comes too often, a device that beeps. The redesign question is whether the building’s routines make a condition manageable without publicity.

The student decides, and the calculus includes things beyond health. A shift, a sibling, a class she is already behind in. Illness becomes the reason offered rather than always the reason operating — and treating that as deception misreads it. It is often the only reason the system accepts.

One adult who knows her situation and doesn’t audit it is the version that works here. Going through the household may be exactly what breaks it at this band.

Evidence

Asthma alone accounts for roughly 13–14 million missed school days per year in the US. Demonstrated as a magnitude.

Home-based multi-trigger asthma intervention returns about 12.3 school days per student per year. (Community Preventive Services Task Force, 2011.) Demonstrated — and note what it demonstrates: the effective intervention happened in the home, not the school. The school’s role in this finding is connection.

NCHS 2024: 5.8% of children ages 5–17 have health-related chronic absence — 14.8% among children with disabilities versus 4.4% among children without. Demonstrated.

CDC: roughly 3 million children missed 15 or more school days per year for health reasons in 2022. Demonstrated.

The AAP establishes the relationship as bidirectional — poor health causes absence, and chronic absence predicts worse long-term health outcomes. Demonstrated, and it matters for how this factor is read: this is a loop, not a one-way barrier.

Lee (2023), School nurses and chronic absenteeism in schools, Journal of School Nursing — students with many partial-day absences proactively seek out school nurses as a source of comfort and support, creating an intervention opening before chronic absenteeism establishes. Qualitative — it establishes the access point, not a prediction. Demonstrated as an access point; Reasoned as a lever.

That re-entry design affects subsequent attendance — no study located establishes this. Proposed. It follows from the restorative reading and from what is demonstrated about exclusion producing more of what it corrects, and nothing here tests it directly.

Honest limit: the health-absence literature is strong on magnitude and nearly silent on what a school does about it. It establishes that illness removes millions of days and that clinical intervention returns some of them. It does not establish that any school-side practice — re-entry procedure, nurse access, family contact — changes a health-related attendance trajectory. The connecting role this row assigns to the school is reasoned from the boundary, not measured.
