Missouri Standard 5.1 · Feedback

Third-Point Feedback

Direct feedback at the work, not the student — look at it together rather than across from each other. The same observation about the same problem lands differently when both people are looking at the same object.

What problem does this solve?

  • Feedback addressed to a student's identity ("you got this wrong") activates defense rather than revision.
  • The student is looking at the teacher's face to read whether they're in trouble, not at the work to understand what to fix.
  • Even specific, well-intentioned corrective feedback can collapse into "this person is evaluating me," which is an entirely different cognitive task than "what needs to change here."
  • Performance-based feedback — grades, scores, praise — ends attention rather than redirecting it.

Why does it work?

The effect comes from where attention lands. When both people are looking at the work, the feedback is about the work, not the person who made it. Potential mechanisms include:

  • Directing attention to a shared external object reduces the threat perception that shuts down revision. The student is reading the work with the teacher rather than being read by the teacher.
  • Looking at the same thing puts the teacher and student in the same role: two people examining an object. The power differential doesn't disappear but it shifts toward collaboration.
  • Specific feedback about the work gives the student something to do. Evaluative feedback about the student gives them something to feel. Only one of those drives revision. (SoL Q5 — practice requires the student's active response, not just receipt of information.)
  • Feedback received from a person you trust travels differently than feedback received from a person you're trying to manage. Relationship is what makes the channel work. (See Relationships.)

What conditions must exist?

  • There is something to look at together: a draft, a problem, a piece of work. This requires enough structure that students are producing something before the conversation, not only after.
  • The feedback is about the work, not the student who made it — "this part isn't clear" versus "you weren't clear here." The grammatical subject matters.
  • The teacher is genuinely looking and responding to what's there, not reading from a rubric while standing beside the student. Performed presence reads as performed presence.
  • The student has enough relationship with the teacher to hear correction as information rather than verdict. Feedback works through relationship; it doesn't substitute for it.

The common misconception is that this is about tone — being kind when delivering feedback. The actual design principle: where both people's eyes are pointed is more important than how softly the words are delivered.

How you'll know it's working

The tell that it's working isn't agreement. It's the student looking at the work instead of at your face.

By grade band

Physically crouch to the student's level; point to the specific part of the work (a letter, a drawing, a number); ask "what did you want this part to do?" rather than telling what it doesn't.

Teach students to read their own work before the teacher conference: "find the place where you got stuck." The student points first; the teacher responds to where the student's attention already is.

Separate revision from evaluation: a draft conversation is a different kind of conversation than a grading conversation, and students need to know which one this is before it starts. Mixing them produces only the defensive response to evaluation.

Other situations

Written feedback only: the same principle applies to margin comments. "This paragraph doesn't support the claim" is aimed at the work. "You're not supporting your claims" is aimed at the student. The grammatical subject is doing the same work it does in a side-by-side conversation.

Evidence

The most direct evidence base is Hattie & Timperley's 2007 framework distinguishing feedback about task, process, self-regulation, and self. Task and process feedback drive improvement; self-level feedback (positive or negative) tends to end attention rather than redirect it. Effect sizes for feedback are high but with enormous variance — the variance is explained almost entirely by the type of feedback, not its presence or absence.

The threat-response mechanism is drawn from the more recent affective-neuroscience work linking evaluation threat with reduced working memory and narrowed attention — exactly the opposite of what's needed for revision. (SoL Q2.)

Honest limit: "third point" is a relational frame, not a measured technique. The studies above support the underlying mechanism; the specific move is the practitioner implementation of it.

Translation notes

Teachers often hear this as a softening strategy: "deliver criticism gently." The mechanism isn't about tone — it's about where both people's attention is. A kind comment about a student's work that places the student as the subject ("you're not quite there") still directs attention at the person. A blunt observation about the work that places the work as the subject ("this sentence contradicts the one above it") still directs attention at the thing that can be changed.
