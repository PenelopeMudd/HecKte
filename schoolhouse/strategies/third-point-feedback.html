Missouri Standard 5.1 · Feedback

Third Point Feedback

Aim the feedback at a shared external reference — a rubric, a model, the criteria, the work itself — rather than at the student. The goal is not softer criticism; the goal is that the student can evaluate the work without defending themselves.

What problem does this solve?

  • Accurate feedback gets rejected — the student hears a judgment about them and stops listening before the useful part.
  • A conference turns into reassurance because the honest version would feel like an attack.
  • Students can't tell the difference between "this draft isn't there yet" and "you aren't good at this."
  • Revision doesn't happen, because accepting the feedback would mean accepting the verdict that came with it.

Why does it work?

The effect comes from where the student's attention is pointed during the exchange. Feedback aimed at a person requires them to hold their own standing while processing information. Feedback aimed at a shared object doesn't. Potential mechanisms include:

  • Ego-threat feedback is rejected regardless of accuracy. Being right isn't sufficient; the feedback has to be receivable. (SoL Q2.)
  • Feedback travels on relationships — the same words land as help or as threat depending on what's already between teacher and student. The third point lowers what the relationship has to carry.
  • Naming the gap between the work and a visible criterion gives the student goal, current position, and next step — the three things effective feedback supplies. (Hattie & Timperley, 2007.)
  • It's the same structure as naming the deed, not the doer — the person stays separable from the thing being evaluated, which is what keeps them reachable.
  • Looking at a shared object together positions the teacher beside the student rather than across from them, which changes what the exchange feels like before anything is said.

What conditions must exist?

  • The criterion exists and the student can see it. Without a visible standard there is no third point — you're just describing your opinion at a distance.
  • The criterion is specific enough to point at. "Good writing" isn't a third point; "the claim appears in the first paragraph" is.
  • There's a real chance to act on it. Feedback with no revision opportunity is a verdict with extra steps.
  • Enough relationship exists to be honest. The third point reduces threat; it doesn't eliminate the need for trust.

The common misconception is that this is a gentler way to deliver criticism — a softening technique. The actual design principle: feedback is only useful if it can be received. Point at the work, not the person, so the student can look at it without defending themselves.

By grade band

The third point is concrete and physical — the actual paper, a picture of what "finished" looks like, a two-item checklist. Acknowledge the process out loud, pointed at the work: "look what happened when you kept going."

Peer feedback with stems, aimed at criteria rather than at each other — here's what you did · why it matters · you might try. The stems are what keep it third-point.

Students self-assess against the criteria first, then the conference compares the two readings. Privacy matters — feedback delivered where peers can hear it re-introduces the ego threat the move exists to remove.

Other situations

When the feedback is behavioral rather than academic: the third point is the agreement or the norm the class authored. "Let's look at what we said about work time." (See Co-Created Norms.)

Evidence

Feedback is among the highest-leverage moves available to a teacher, and also one of the most variable — the same feedback produces gains or does nothing depending on how it's received. (Hattie & Timperley, 2007.) That variability is the reason this atom exists: the delivery isn't a nicety layered on top of the content, it's part of whether the content functions at all.

Worth naming honestly: "third point" is a practitioner framing rather than a named research construct. What's evidence-backed is the mechanism underneath — ego threat blocks uptake, and task-focused feedback outperforms person-focused feedback. The third point is a reliable way to get there, not a studied intervention in its own right.

Translation notes

Teachers often hear this as a politeness protocol — say it about the work, not the kid, and you've been kind. The mechanism isn't kindness. It's that a student defending their standing has no capacity left to evaluate their draft, and the third point is what frees it up. Being gentle without a shared criterion doesn't accomplish this; being direct with one usually does.

The related misread is that third-point feedback should be softer. It can be considerably more direct than person-aimed feedback, because there's nothing to soften — the gap between the work and the standard is just information.

The tell that it's working isn't agreement. It's the student looking at the work instead of at your face.
