Missouri Standard 5.3 · Repair

The Repair Conversation

A short, structured conversation after harm, built on four questions that surface what happened and what would make it right — held with each person separately first, then together when both are ready. The goal is not an apology; the goal is that the student stays inside the community that has to keep working after the incident.

What problem does this solve?

  • Something happened and the room can't just proceed as if it didn't.
  • The available responses feel like a choice between letting it go and removing someone — neither addresses the harm.
  • A forced apology gets produced and nothing about tomorrow is different.
  • The student who caused harm leaves knowing they're in trouble but not what they'd do differently.

Why does it work?

The effect comes from who does the accounting. In a consequence, the adult names the wrong and assigns the cost. In a repair conversation, the student constructs the account — that's the part that changes behavior, and the part removal skips entirely. Potential mechanisms include:

  • "What happened?" opens a story; "Why did you do that?" invites a defense. The first question gets information, the second gets justification. Same intent, opposite result — the wording is doing the work.
  • Naming who was affected makes the harm concrete and external, so the student is looking at an impact rather than defending an identity.
  • The student stays inside relationships and belonging — the channel any future correction has to travel through. Removal solves the moment by removing the mechanism.
  • Staying reachable keeps feedback and instruction working; a student who's been excluded is harder to teach for weeks afterward, not just that hour. (SoL Q2.)

What conditions must exist?

It won't hold as a one-off pulled out after a blowup:

  • Relationship is already banked. A repair conversation spends relational credit; if none was deposited, there's nothing to spend.
  • Both people are regulated enough to talk. Held too soon, it becomes a second incident.
  • Each person is met separately first — the joint conversation only works if both arrive already able to hear the other.
  • The teacher can sit with silence and not fill it. Most of the useful thinking happens in the pause after a question.
  • The agreement reached is small enough to actually happen and gets followed up on. An unkept repair teaches that the process is decorative.
  • The whole-school context isn't undercutting it — the practice underperforms when used reactively and in isolation.

The common misconception is that this is the soft alternative to a consequence. The actual design principle: harm is the problem to be addressed, not the person. The response has to keep the student inside the community that has to metabolize what happened.

How you'll know it's working

The tell that it's working isn't the apology. It's whether the two of them can work together next week.

By grade band

Two questions, not four — what happened and what would help. Concrete restitution the child can physically do (rebuild the tower, help clean up). A designated peace corner where the conversation happens, so the place carries part of the routine.

All four questions, teacher-facilitated. Introduce the apology-of-action — doing something rather than saying something. Students begin holding these with light support.

Students can facilitate for peers once they've been through several. Affective statements ("when that happened, I felt…") in place of accusation. Privacy matters more at this age than at any other — a repair conversation held where peers can watch will fail on audience alone.

Other situations

Teacher-and-student repair: the same four questions when the rupture is between you and a student, including your own account. This is the version teachers skip and the one students remember.

Evidence

Exclusionary discipline is associated with increased future misbehavior and dropout risk — the response intended to correct behavior predicts more of it. That finding is what makes repair a practical argument rather than a moral one. (Klevan 2021.)

The strongest honest caution in this literature is about fidelity, not efficacy: a restorative conversation run once, after a blowup, with no prior relational deposit, is not the same intervention as a practice running all year — and shouldn't be judged as one. (Klevan 2021.)

A named limit worth keeping: a structured process does not guarantee that every voice is genuinely heard. Formal equality in turn-taking and actual equity of participation are different things.

Translation notes

Teachers often read this as a script — four questions, asked in order, incident closed. The questions aren't the intervention; they're a shape that keeps the adult from doing the accounting for the student. A teacher who asks all four and then supplies the answers has run the form of it and none of the substance.

Repair doesn't mean no consequence. It means the consequence is doing something about the harm rather than being removed from the room where it happened.
