Missouri Standard 5.2 · Visual Supports

Post What You'd Otherwise Repeat

Put on the wall only what students will actually look at during the lesson — directions, steps, vocabulary, sentence frames — so the teacher stops repeating it and students stop holding it in working memory. A posted support earns its wall space by getting used; everything else is wallpaper.

What problem does this solve?

  • The teacher is the walking reference card — students interrupt constantly because the directions, the vocabulary, the steps only live in the teacher's voice.
  • Working memory is carrying logistics (what step am I on, how do I spell this word, what's the format) that a display could carry instead.
  • Students who process more slowly, who are learning the language, or who lost the thread mid-task have no way back in without asking.
  • The wall is full, which means nothing on it is findable.

Why does it work?

The effect comes from offloading. A posted support holds information the student would otherwise have to hold internally. Potential mechanisms include:

  • Working memory has a small capacity that is shared between storing and processing. Every item shifted from internal storage to an external display frees capacity for the actual work. The most important moment to do this is when the task is hardest — exactly when load peaks. (SoL: managing load is a prerequisite for learning.)
  • A reference that's visually present but unneeded costs almost nothing; a reference that's absent when needed costs a hand raised, a wait, an interruption, and often a lost thought.
  • Students who are learning the language or building fluency use supports differently than native speakers: sentence frames and vocabulary displays aren't scaffolds to remove, they're conditions under which harder thinking becomes possible.
  • A word wall that's actually used is different in kind from a word wall that decorates: the difference is whether students have been taught to look at it and whether looking at it actually answers the question they have.

What conditions must exist?

  • The display is legible at the distance students actually use it from — font size matters, not visual interest.
  • Students have been taught to use it: there's a practiced move of "look at the wall before you ask me."
  • The wall is curated enough that the relevant thing can be found. More than five to seven items on a wall is usually too many to be usable.
  • What's posted reflects what today's lesson actually needs, not what last month's unit needed.
  • The support stays up as long as the content is live, then comes down.

The common misconception is treating posted supports as evidence of teaching — a documented wall for an observation. The actual design principle: if students aren't looking at it, it isn't a support. It's decoration that happens to have words on it.

How you'll know it's working

The tell that it's working isn't a full wall. It's students looking at something instead of looking at you.

By grade band

Picture + word pairs for vocabulary; a posted routine with icons rather than only text; one direction at a time, not a numbered list. Point to it explicitly when referring to it.

Sentence frames for discussions and writing; a posted steps checklist for complex tasks; vocabulary anchored to the current unit's key ideas, not a comprehensive word wall.

Academic language targets; criteria for complex tasks; the most commonly misapplied disciplinary concept of the unit. At this age, a display that's always up becomes invisible — rotate or add a signal that directs attention to it.

Other situations

Digital equivalent: a slide left on the board, a pinned Docs comment, a persistent reference section in a digital notebook. Same mechanism, different surface.

Evidence

The load-reduction mechanism is drawn from cognitive load theory — specifically the finding that working memory is capacity-limited and that reducing extraneous load (holding routine information) preserves capacity for germane load (the actual work). (Deans for Impact, SoL 2nd ed. 2026, Q1.)

The claim about English learners and language learners isn't a hedge — it's a finding that scaffolds increase access to higher-order tasks for students building language fluency, not a substitute for them.

The honest caveat: the existence of a posted support does not cause load reduction. Students have to be taught to use it and the display has to be legible and findable. A cluttered or never-referenced wall has approximately zero cognitive effect.

Translation notes

Teachers hear this as a compliance item — word wall required, learning objective posted. The mechanism doesn't come from posting; it comes from using. The question isn't "is it on the wall?" It's "did a student look at it today instead of looking at me?"
