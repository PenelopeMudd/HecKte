Missouri Standard 5.2 · Visual Supports

Post What You’d Otherwise Repeat

Make visible the information students would otherwise have to hold in their heads or ask you for — the sequence, the schedule, the steps, the criteria. The goal is not a decorated wall; the goal is that the room carries the information so working memory doesn't have to.

What problem does this solve?

  • The same question gets asked five times an hour about something already said.
  • Students who missed the verbal instruction have no way to recover it except asking.
  • A multi-step task stalls halfway because the middle steps have fallen out of memory.
  • Transitions require the teacher to narrate what's next, every time.

Why does it work?

The effect comes from the information being externally available at the moment of need, not from the visual existing. Potential mechanisms include:

  • A posted sequence is external memory — the steps are read rather than recalled, which returns working memory to the actual task. Load drops at exactly the point it usually spikes. (SoL Q1.)
  • Information presented both visually and verbally is better supported than either alone — the posted version isn't a duplicate of what you said, it's the second channel.
  • A visible schedule activates prior knowledge and makes the day anticipatable, which frees attention rather than spending it on uncertainty.
  • Predictability is a safety condition: a student who can see what's coming isn't managing threat, and a student who lost the thread can recover without a public admission. That's a belonging mechanism, not just an efficiency one. (Klevan 2021.)

What conditions must exist?

  • The content was taught first. A posted chart references something students have learned; it can't do the teaching. Posting a routine that was never rehearsed just decorates the wall.
  • It's readable from where students sit, in the format they can actually use — pictures where words won't land.
  • It stays current. A schedule that's wrong twice teaches students to stop looking at it.
  • The wall isn't full. Every additional item competes for the same attentional gate — a room where everything is posted is a room where nothing is legible.
  • Students are pointed to it rather than answered. "Check the chart" instead of the answer is what makes it a resource rather than wallpaper.

The common misconception is making the room look organized and print-rich. The actual design principle: anything you'd have to say more than twice should be visible instead.

By grade band

Picture schedules and icon cues — images carry what text can't yet. Visual routine charts at child eye level. The picture is the instruction, not a caption on it.

Anchor charts co-built with students during the lesson; posted step sequences; a visible agenda for the day.

Other situations

Border case worth knowing: the chart is a designed system; co-building it with students is live. Same artifact, two different moves.

Evidence

The load and dual-representation mechanisms are well supported in the learning-sciences literature. What is not supported is the stronger version this often becomes — that a print-rich environment improves learning on its own. The opposite risk is real: visual clutter competes for attention, and a heavily decorated room can cost more than it gives, particularly for younger students.

The honest claim is narrow and useful: a specific visual, referencing taught content, available at the moment of need, reduces load. A wall full of visuals is a different thing and shouldn't borrow this evidence.

Translation notes

Teachers often read this as "post more things," which produces the exact clutter that undermines the mechanism. Posting is not free — every item spends some of the room's limited attention budget. The move is selective: what would I otherwise have to repeat?

The other misread is treating the chart as the teaching. A posted routine reminds; it doesn't install. If students can't do the thing, the answer is rehearsal, not a bigger sign. (See Teach the Transition.)

The tell that it's working isn't a full wall. It's students looking at something instead of looking at you.
